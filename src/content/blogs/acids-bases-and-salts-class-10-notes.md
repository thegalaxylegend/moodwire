## <a id="-formula-bank"></a>⚡ Formula Bank

### <a id="acid-and-base-formulas"></a>**Acid and Base Formulas**

- **pH of a strong acid:** $pH = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ — where $[H^+]$ is the concentration of hydrogen ions in moles per liter

- **pH of a strong base:** $pOH = -\log_{10}[OH^-]$ — where $[OH^-]$ is the concentration of hydroxide ions in moles per liter

- **Relationship between pH and pOH:** pH + pOH = $14 — at room temperature$

- **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:** $pH = PK_a + \log_{10}\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$ — where pK_an is the acid dissociation constant, $[A^-]$ is the concentration of conjugate base, and $[HA]$ is the concentration of weak acid
Examiner's Trap: Students often forget to consider the temperature when using the relationship between pH and pOH.

### <a id="salt-formulas"></a>**Salt Formulas**

- **Formation of a salt:** $acid + Base \rightarrow Salt + Water$ — general equation for salt formation

- **Solubility product constant ($K_sp$):** OK_{sp} = [A^+][B^-]$ — where $[A^+]$ and $[B^-]$ are the concentrations of the ions in a saturated solution$

- **Common ion effect:** OK_{sp} = [A^+][B^-]$ — where the presence of a common ion affects the solubility of a salt$
Examiner's Trap: Candidates often confuse the solubility product constant (DSP) with the acid dissociation constant (Ka).

### <a id="titration-formulas"></a>**Titration Formulas**

- **Titration equation:** $acid + Base \rightarrow Salt + Water$ — equation for the reaction between an acid and a base during titration

- **Number of moles of acid:** $in = \frac{m}{M}$ — where and is the number of moles, me is the mass, and ME is the molar mass of the acid

- **Number of moles of base:** $in = \frac{m}{M}$ — where and is the number of moles, me is the mass, and ME is the molar mass of the base

- **Molarity of a solution:** $KM = \frac{n}{V}$ — where ME is the molarity, and is the number of moles, and DVD is the volume of the solution in liters
Examiner's Trap: Students often forget to calculate the number of moles of the acid or base before proceeding with the titration calculation.

### <a id="concentration-formulas"></a>**Concentration Formulas**

- **Molarity (M):** $KM = \frac{n}{V}$ — where and is the number of moles and DVD is the volume of the solution in liters

- **Molality (m):** $km = \frac{n}{w}$ — where and is the number of moles, and we are the mass of the solvent in kilograms

- **Normality (N):** $IN = \frac{n}{V}$ — where and is the number of equivalents and DVD is the volume of the solution in liters

- **Mass percentage:** $\frac{m}{M} \times 100\%$ — where me is the mass of the solute and ME is the mass of the solution
Examiner's Trap: Candidates often confuse molarity with molality or normality.

### <a id="equivalence-formulas"></a>**Equivalence Formulas**

- **Equivalent weight:** $HE = \frac{M}{n}$ — where HE is the equivalent weight, ME is the molar mass, and and is the number of replaceable hydrogen atoms

- **Number of equivalents:** $in = \frac{m}{E}$ — where and is the number of equivalents, me is the mass, and HE is the equivalent weight
Examiner's Trap: Students often forget to consider the number of replaceable hydrogen atoms when calculating the equivalent weight.

- **Mistake 1 — Incorrect pH Scale:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** pH $= 14$ 

- pOH without considering the correct pH range
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** pH $= $

- $\log_{10}[\text{H}^{+}]$ and understanding that pH + pOH = 14
 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Remember that pH + pOH = $14 and pH ranges from 0 to $14

- **Mistake 2 — Weak and Strong Acids/Bases:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** All acids/bases are strong
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Understanding the difference between strong acids/bases (completely ionized) and weak acids/bases (partially ionized)
 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1 mark
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Recall that strong acids/bases are HCl, OH_{2}SO_{4}$, NaOH, and KOH, while weak acids/bases are $CH_{3}COOL$ and NH_{4}$OH

- **Mistake 3 — Salt Formation:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** All salts are formed by a strong acid and a strong base
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Understanding that salts can be formed by a strong acid and a weak base or a weak acid and a strong base
 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Remember that salts can be formed by various combinations of strong and weak acids/bases, such as NaCl from HCl and NaOH

- **Mistake 4 — Neutralization Reaction:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** Neutralization reaction involves only strong acids/bases
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Understanding that neutralization [reactions](/blog/chemical-reactions-and-equations-class-10-notes) involve both strong and weak acids/bases
 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1 mark
 

- $🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Recall that neutralization reactions involve the combination of an acid and a base to form a salt and water, such as HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_{2}OF$

- **Mistake 5 — Ionization of Acids/Bases:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** Acids/bases do not ionize in water
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Understanding the ionization of acids/bases in water, such as OH_{2}O + HCl $\rightarrow$H_{3}O^{+} + Cl^{-}
 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 3 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Remember that acids/bases ionize in water to produce OH^{+}$ or $OH^{-}$ ions, such as HCl \rightarrow H^{+} + Cl^{-}$ and NaOH $\rightarrow$Na^{+} + OH^{-}

#### Q1 (2020 CBSE): 
What is the nature of the solution of RNA_2CO_$3 in water? $

- 🪤 **Trap:** Most students forget that RNA_2CO_$3 is a salt of a strong base and a weak acid, which makes the solution basic.$

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Write the chemical equation for the dissolution of RNA_2CO_$3 in water → Na_2CO_3 + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2CO_$3
 Step 2: Identify the products of the reaction, which include NaOH, a strong base.
 **Final Answer:** $\text{Basic}$

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** Recall that salts of strong bases and weak acids produce basic solutions.

---
#### Q2 (2019 CBSE): 
A student drops HCl in NaOH solution. The student measures the initial and final temperatures of the solution. If the initial temperature is $25^{\circ}CD$ and the final temperature is $30^{\circ}CD$, what$ can be said about the reaction?$

- 🪤 **Trap:** Many students assume the reaction is endothermic because the temperature increased.

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Recall that the reaction between HCl and NaOH is exothermic → HCl + NaOH $\rightarrow$NaCl + $H_2O$
 Step 2: Understand that the increase in temperature indicates the release of heat energy → HQ = MC\delta TO, where $\delta TO$ is positive.
 **Final Answer:** $\text{Exothermic}$

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** Remember that neutralization [reactions](/blog/chemical-reactions-and-equations-class-10-notes) between acids and bases are exothermic.

---
#### Q3 (2018 CBSE): 
What is the pH of a solution of NaOH with concentration $0.01 \text{ MOL/L}$?

- 🪤 **Trap:** Students often forget to use the correct formula for pOH before calculating pH.

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Calculate pOH using the formula pOH = -\log[OH^-]$ → pOH = -\log(0.01)$
 Step 2: Calculate pH using the relationship pH + pOH = $14 → pH = 14 - pOH$
 **Final Answer:** pH = 14 - (-($\log(0.01))) = 14 - (-2) = 12$

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** Recall that for strong bases, $[OH^-] = \text{concentration$ of base}$, and use pH + pOH = 14$ to find pH.

## <a id="-the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

### <a id="the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong
The concept of acids, bases, and salts can be tricky, and many students fall into common misconceptions.

- **The misconception (what 85% believe):** Most students think that acids are just substances that are sour in taste and bases are substances that are bitter.

- **The reality (what 99% know):** The reality is that acids are substances that donate OH^{+}$ ions, while bases are substances that accept OH^{+}$ ions or donate $OH^{-}$ ions.

- The correct definition of acids and bases is based on the Arrhenius theory, which states that acids increase the concentration of OH^{+}$ ions in a solution, while bases increase the concentration of $OH^{-}$ ions.$

### <a id="the-diagnostic-question"></a>The Diagnostic Question
Consider the following question to diagnose your understanding:
What is the nature of $NH_{4}CLR$ in water?

- If you answered: $NH_{4}CLR$ is a strong acid → fix: $NH_{4}CLR$ is a salt that gives an acidic solution due to the hydrolysis of $NH_{4}^{+}$ ions.

- If you answered: $NH_{4}CLR$ is a salt that gives an acidic solution due to the hydrolysis of $NH_{4}^{+}$ ions → now extend this: The hydrolysis of $NH_{4}^{+}$ ions can be represented by the equation $NH_{4}^{+} + H_{2}O \rightleftharpoons NH_{3} + H_{3}O^{+}$, which shows that $NH_{4}CLR$ is a salt of a weak base and a strong acid.

### <a id="how-to-never-forget-this"></a>How to Never Forget This
To remember the difference between acids and bases, you can use the following mnemonic:

- Acids: **A**always **D**donate OH^{+}$ ions$

- Bases: **B**cases **A**always accept OH^{+}$ ions or **D**donate $OH^{-}$ ions$
You can also use a visual analogy, such as a seesaw, to represent the equilibrium between acids and bases. The OH^{+}$ ions and $OH^{-}$ ions are on opposite sides of the seesaw, and when one side goes up, the other side goes down. This represents the fact that when the concentration of OH^{+}$ ions increases, the concentration of $OH^{-}$ ions decreases, and vice versa.

### <a id="key-formulas"></a>Key Formulas
Some key formulas to remember:

- $\text{pH} = -\log[H^{+}]$

- $\text{pOH} = -\log[OH^{-}]$

- pH + pOH = 14

- OK_{w} = [H^{+}][OH^{-}] = 10^{-14}

### <a id="common-mistakes"></a>Common Mistakes
Some common mistakes to avoid:

- Confusing strong acids with weak acids

- Confusing strong bases with weak bases

- Not understanding the concept of hydrolysis and its effect on the pH of a solution

- Not being able to calculate the pH of a solution using the given concentration of OH^{+}$ or $OH^{-}$ ions.$
