## <a id="-formula-bank"></a>⚡ Formula Bank

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-ability"></a>Formulas for Modals of Ability

- **Present Ability:** can + base form of verb (e.g. can speak) — shows ability to do something

- **Past Ability:** could + base form of verb (e.g. could speak) — shows past ability to do something

- **Future Ability:** will be able to + base form of verb (e.g. will be able to speak) — shows future ability to do something
Examiner's Trap: Be careful with the verb forms and tenses when using modals of ability.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-permission"></a>Formulas for Modals of Permission

- **Present Permission:** can + base form of verb (e.g. can go) — shows permission to do something

- **Past Permission:** could + base form of verb (e.g. could go) — shows past permission to do something

- **Future Permission:** may + base form of verb (e.g. may go) — shows future permission to do something
Examiner's Trap: Make sure to distinguish between modals of permission and modals of ability.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-obligation"></a>Formulas for Modals of Obligation

- **Present Obligation:** must + base form of verb (e.g. must go) — shows necessity to do something

- **Past Obligation:** had to + base form of verb (e.g. had to go) — shows past necessity to do something

- **Future Obligation:** will have to + base form of verb (e.g. will have to go) — shows future necessity to do something
Examiner's Trap: Be aware of the differences between modals of obligation and modals of advice.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-advice"></a>Formulas for Modals of Advice

- **Present Advice:** should + base form of verb (e.g. should go) — shows recommendation to do something

- **Past Advice:** should have + past participle of verb (e.g. should have gone) — shows past recommendation to do something

- **Future Advice:** ought to + base form of verb (e.g. ought to go) — shows future recommendation to do something
Examiner's Trap: Pay attention to the verb forms and tenses when using modals of advice.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-certainty"></a>Formulas for Modals of Certainty

- **Present Certainty:** must + base form of verb (e.g. must be) — shows complete certainty about something

- **Past Certainty:** must have + past participle of verb (e.g. must have been) — shows past certainty about something

- **Future Certainty:** will + base form of verb (e.g. will be) — shows future certainty about something
Examiner's Trap: Be careful not to confuse modals of certainty with modals of obligation.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-doubt"></a>Formulas for Modals of Doubt

- **Present Doubt:** may + base form of verb (e.g. may be) — shows possibility about something

- **Past Doubt:** may have + past participle of verb (e.g. may have been) — shows past possibility about something

- **Future Doubt:** might + base form of verb (e.g. might be) — shows future possibility about something
Examiner's Trap: Make sure to distinguish between modals of doubt and modals of certainty.

### <a id="formulas-for-modals-of-necessity"></a>Formulas for Modals of Necessity

- **Present Necessity:** need to + base form of verb (e.g. need to go) — shows necessity to do something

- **Past Necessity:** needed to + base form of verb (e.g. needed to go) — shows past necessity to do something

- **Future Necessity:** will need to + base form of verb (e.g. will need to go) — shows future necessity to do something
Examiner's Trap: Be aware of the differences between modals of necessity and modals of obligation.

## <a id="-the-5-mistakes-that-cost-marks"></a>🪤 The 5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

### <a id="-the-5-mistakes-that-cost-marks"></a>🪤 The 5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

- **Mistake 1 — Missing Articles:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** She can 🎤 sing. (without an article)
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** She can **the** 🎤 sing. (rarely needed, but sometimes) or more commonly, She can sing **a** song.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1 mark
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Always check if the sentence needs an article (a, an, the) before the main verb.

- **Mistake 2 — Incorrect Modal Verb Usage:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** I **must** go to market. (without considering alternatives)
 

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** 
 

- **Must** (for strong obligation): You **must** complete your homework.

- **Should** (for advice): You **should** try this cake.

- **Can** (for ability): I **can** swim.

- **May** (for permission): You **may** go now.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Match the modal verb with its correct usage: obligation (must), advice (should), ability (can), permission (may).

- **Mistake 3 — Double Negatives:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** I **can't** do **nothing**.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** I **can't** do **anything**. or I **can** do **nothing**.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** A double negative makes a positive; make sure to use one negative.

- **Mistake 4 — Forgetting the Correct Form of the Verb:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** I **can** to go.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** I **can** go.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1–2 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** After a modal verb, use the **base form** of the verb (no -ing, no -ed).

- **Mistake 5 — Misusing Modal Verbs for Deductions:**
 

- 🔴 **What students write:** He **must** be at home; I **saw** him.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** 
 

- For **logical conclusions**: He **must** be at home; his car is here.

- For **past deductions**: He **must have** eaten; he's full.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2–3 marks
 

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** Use **must have** for past deductions and **must** for present logical conclusions.

## <a id="-3-solved-pyqs"></a>✏️ 3 Solved PYQs

### <a id="-3-solved-pyqs"></a>✏️ 3 Solved PYQs

**Q1 (2026 CBSE):** Fill in the blank with the correct modal: "You ______ be very tired after working all day."

- 🪤 **Trap:** Students often confuse "must" and "might".

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Understand the context of the sentence, which implies a high probability of being tired.
 Step 2: Choose the modal that best fits the context. "Must" is used for logical conclusions or strong probability.
 **Final Answer:** must

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** Quickly identify the context and use "must" for strong probability.

---
**Q2 (2019 CBSE):** Choose the correct modal: "If I ______ you earlier, I would have invited you to the party."

- 🪤 **Trap:** Students often confuse "had known" with "would have known".

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Identify the sentence as a third conditional sentence, which requires "had + past participle" in the if-clause.
 Step 2: Select the correct modal or verb form. For third conditional, we use "had" + past participle.
 **Final Answer:** had known

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** Recognize third conditional sentences and use "had" + past participle.

---
**Q3 (2020 CBSE):** Fill in the blank: "Students ______ respect their teachers."

- 🪤 **Trap:** Students may incorrectly use "can" instead of a more suitable modal.

- 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**
 Step 1: Understand the sentence implies a general rule or obligation.
 Step 2: Choose a modal that expresses obligation or necessity. "Must" or "should" can be used for such sentences.
 **Final Answer:** should

- ⚡ **Speed trick:** For general rules, use "should" or "must".

## <a id="-the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

### <a id="the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

- **The misconception (what 85% believe):** Most students think that modals like **can**, **could**, **may**, and **might** are interchangeable and can be used in any situation to express possibility or ability.

- **The reality (what 99% know):** 
 

- **Can** is used for ability or capability (e.g.

- I **can** speak English).

- **Could** is the past form of **can** and is used for past ability (e.g.

- I **could** swim when I was younger).

- **May** and **might** both express possibility but **might** implies a lower level of possibility (e.g.

- It **may** rain today; He **might** come to the party).

### <a id="diagnostic-question"></a>Diagnostic Question

- If you answered **B**: you have the misconception → fix: Remember **can** is for ability and **may** for possibility.

- Question: Which of the following sentences uses **can** correctly?

- A) She **can** attend the meeting; it is likely.

- B) She **can** attend the meeting; she has the ability.

- C) She **can** attend the meeting; she might.

- D) She **can** attend the meeting; she is likely to.

- If you answered **B**: you are in the top 5% → now extend this: Use **can** for general abilities and **be able to** for specific situations (e.g.

- I **can** drive but I **am able to** drive a manual car).

### <a id="how-to-never-forget-this"></a>How to Never Forget This

- Create a table with four columns: **Modal**, **Meaning**, **Example**, and **Note**.
 | Modal | Meaning | Example | Note |
 |---------|-----------------|----------------------|-----------------------------|
 | **Can** | Ability | I **can** swim. | For general abilities |
 | **Could**| Past ability | I **could** dance. | Past form of **can** |
 | **May** | Possibility | It **may** rain. | For possibilities |
 | **Might**| Lower possibility| He **might** come. | Implies lower possibility |

- Visualize a story where **Can** is a person with superpowers (ability), **Could** is their past self, **May** is a weather forecast (possibility), and **Might** is a less certain forecast.

### <a id="key-points-to-remember"></a>Key Points to Remember

- **Can** and **be able to** are not always interchangeable; **can** is used for general abilities.

- **Could** has two main uses: past ability and conditional sentences.

- **May** and **might** both express possibility but with different levels of certainty.

### <a id="common-mistakes-in-using-modals"></a>Common Mistakes in Using Modals

- Using **can** for possibility (e.g.

- It **can** rain today).

- Using **may** and **might** interchangeably without considering the level of possibility.

### <a id="tips-for-the-exam"></a>Tips for the Exam

- Read the sentences carefully and understand the context.

- Identify the meaning you want to express: ability, possibility, or past ability.

- Choose the correct modal based on the context and meaning.

## <a id="-last-5-minutes-box"></a>🔁 Last 5 Minutes Box

### <a id="-core-formulas"></a>⚡ Core Formulas

- can/could + verb — gives you ability or possibility

- may/might + verb — gives you permission or possibility

- shall/should + verb — gives you advice or recommendation

- will/would + verb — gives you future prediction or intention

- must + verb — gives you necessity or obligation

### <a id="-mustknow-facts"></a>🧠 Must-Know Facts

- modal verbs are used to express degrees of possibility, ability, and permission

- modal verbs are auxiliary verbs that are used with main verbs to express different meanings

- the most common modal verbs in English are can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, and ought to

### <a id="-never-forget"></a>🚫 Never Forget

- ❌ assuming that can and may are always interchangeable → ✅ using can for ability and may for permission

- ❌ using shall for all future predictions → ✅ using will for future predictions and shall for promises or offers

## <a id="-practice-mcqs"></a>📝 Practice MCQs

**1. If it _____ (rain) yesterday, the crops _____ (not die).**
**A)**    rained, wouldn't have died
**B)**    rains, won't die
**C)**    will rain, don't die
**D)**    rain, didn't die

**Answer:** A) The correct answer is 'rained, wouldn't have died' because it is a third conditional sentence. The third conditional is used for past conditions that did not occur and their probable past consequences. The sentence structure for third conditional is 'if + subject + had + past participle, subject + would + have + past participle'. The other options are incorrect because they do not follow the correct sentence structure for the given condition.

---

**2. You _____ (must/should/can) try to finish your homework on time.**
**A)**    must
**B)**    should
**C)**    can
**D)**    may

**Answer:** B) The correct answer is 'should' because it is used to express advice or recommendation. 'Must' is used for obligation, 'can' is used for ability, and 'may' is used for permission. The sentence is giving advice, so 'should' is the correct modal verb to use.

---

**3. If you _____ (study) for 3 hours, you _____ (pass) the exam.**
**A)**    study, will pass
**B)**    studies, passes
**C)**    will study, pass
**D)**    would study, would pass

**Answer:** A) The correct answer is 'study, will pass' because it is a first conditional sentence. The first conditional is used for future conditions that are likely to occur and their probable future consequences. The sentence structure for first conditional is 'if + present simple, subject + will + base form'. The other options are incorrect because they do not follow the correct sentence structure for the given condition.

---

**4. The teacher _____ (can/may/must) be at the staff room now.**
**A)**    can
**B)**    may
**C)**    must
**D)**    shall

**Answer:** B) The correct answer is 'may' because it is used to express possibility. 'Can' is used for ability, 'must' is used for obligation, and 'shall' is used for future action or obligation. The sentence is expressing possibility, so 'may' is the correct modal verb to use.

---

**5. If I _____ (have) 1000 rupees, I _____ (buy) that phone.**
**A)**    have, will buy
**B)**    had, would buy
**C)**    will have, buy
**D)**    would have, would buy

**Answer:** A) The correct answer is 'have, will buy' because it is a first conditional sentence. The first conditional is used for future conditions that are likely to occur and their probable future consequences. The sentence structure for first conditional is 'if + present simple, subject + will + base form'. The other options are incorrect because they do not follow the correct sentence structure for the given condition.
