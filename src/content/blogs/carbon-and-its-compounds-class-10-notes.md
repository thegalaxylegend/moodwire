## <a id="-the-5-mistakes-that-cost-marks"></a>🪤 The 5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

### <a id="mistake-1-nomenclature-nightmare"></a>**Mistake 1 — Nomenclature Nightmare:**

- 🔴 **What students write:** Confusing the longest carbon chain or incorrect numbering for branched chains and functional groups. For example, naming $\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}(\text{CH}_3)-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3$ as 2-ethylpropane instead of 2-methylbutane. Another common error is missing the position number for functional groups when ambiguity exists, like just 'propanol' instead of 'propan-1-ol' or 'propan-2-ol'.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Correctly identifying the longest continuous carbon chain, numbering from the end that gives the lowest number to the substituent or functional group, and using proper prefixes (e.g.

- 'methyl', 'ethyl') and suffixes (e.g.

- '-ol', '-al', '-oic acid'). For the example above, the correct name is 2-methylbutane.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1 or 2 marks for incorrect IUPAC naming.

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** "Find the longest chain, then number from the end closest to the first branch or functional group. If there's a tie, prioritize functional groups over branches. Practice with the exam compass naming exercises!"

### <a id="mistake-2-isomer-identity-crisis"></a>**Mistake 2 — Isomer Identity Crisis:**

- 🔴 **What students write:** Drawing the same molecule multiple \times by simply rotating it or mirroring it and presenting them as different structural isomers. Or, failing to draw *all* possible structural isomers for a given molecular formula. For instance, for $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$, students might draw two forms of butane and claim they are isomers.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Distinct structural arrangements (different connectivity of atoms) that cannot be superimposed.

- **For $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$, there are only two isomers:** n-butane and 2-methylpropane (isobutane).

- **For $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$, there are three:** pentane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 or 3 marks, especially in questions asking for all possible isomers.

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** "Draw one, then mentally rotate it. If it looks the same, it's not a new isomer. Systematically reduce the main chain length and add branches. Check your work on examcompass.web.app for visual clarity."

### <a id="mistake-3-unbalanced-equation-blunders"></a>**Mistake 3 — Unbalanced Equation Blunders:**

- 🔴 **What students write:** Failing to balance the [chemical](/blog/chemical-reactions-and-equations-class-10-notes) equations, particularly for combustion reactions where oxygen coefficients are frequently incorrect, or for reactions like esterification where water is often missed as a product.

- **Example:** $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (unbalanced).

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Fully balanced [chemical](/blog/chemical-reactions-and-equations-class-10-notes) equations with correct stoichiometric coefficients for all reactants and products.

- **For the example above, the balanced equation is:
 \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} 

- 💸 **Marks lost:** ** 1 or 2 marks for an unbalanced equation.

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** "Balance Carbon (C) first, then Hydrogen (H), then Oxygen (O). Remember 'CHO' order! Double-check atom counts on both sides. Don't forget catalysts or conditions like 'heat' or 'conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$' over the arrow."

### <a id="mistake-4-functional-group-fumbles"></a>**Mistake 4 — Functional Group Fumbles:**

- 🔴 **What students write:** Misidentifying or confusing the functional groups and their corresponding suffixes/prefixes. For instance, calling an aldehyde ($\text{-CHO}$) an alcohol ($\text{-OH}$) or vice versa. Another common mistake is not recognizing the specific arrangement of atoms in functional groups like carboxylic acids ($\text{-COOH}$) or ketones ($\text{>C=O}$).

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** Accurate identification of the functional group based on its characteristic atoms and bonding, and correctly associating it with its homologous series and IUPAC nomenclature.

- Alcohol: $\text{-OH}$ (suffix -ol)
 

- Aldehyde: $\text{-CHO}$ (suffix -al)
 

- Ketone: $\text{>C=O}$ (suffix -one, not at ends of chain)
 

- Carboxylic acid: $\text{-COOH}$ (suffix -oic acid)

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 1 mark for incorrect identification or naming.

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** "Create a quick cheat sheet or flashcards with the functional group structure and its name/suffix. $\text{CHO}$ is aldehyde, $\text{COOH}$ is acid, $\text{OH}$ is alcohol. Test yourself using the 'neet blueprint 2027' style quick quizzes."

### <a id="mistake-5-electron-dot-structure-errors"></a>**Mistake 5 — Electron Dot Structure Errors:**

- 🔴 **What students write:** Incorrectly drawing electron dot structures by either showing too many or too few valence electrons, missing lone pairs, or not satisfying the octet/duplet rule for all atoms. For example, drawing methane ($\text{CH}_4$) without ensuring carbon has 8 electrons in its valence shell (shared) and hydrogen has 2. Or drawing ethene ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$) with single bonds between carbons.

- ✅ **What examiners expect:** All valence electrons shown (dots), correct sharing of electrons to form covalent bonds, and accurate representation of lone pairs (if any). Every atom should satisfy its octet (for C, O) or duplet (for H) rule in the final structure. For $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$, it should show a double bond between carbons.

- 💸 **Marks lost:** 2 or 3 marks for significant errors in electron dot structures.

- 🔧 **The fix (30-second trick):** "Count total valence electrons first. Place the central atom (usually C). Distribute electrons to form single bonds, then complete octets for outer atoms, then the central atom. Convert lone pairs to multiple bonds if needed. Always verify C has 8 electrons and H has 2. Refer to jee compass for clear examples."

- **Q1 (2020 CBSE):** What is the general formula of alkenes?

 - 🧮 **Solution (Step-by-step):**

 Step 2: Apply the degree of unsaturation for alkenes, which reduces the number of hydrogen atoms by 2: $C_nH_{2n}$

 - 🪤 **Trap:** Students often misplace the double bond or forget to number the carbon chain correctly.

## <a id="-the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

### <a id="the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong
When it comes to Carbon and its Compounds, the majority of students have a flawed understanding of the concept of allotropy in carbon. 

### <a id="the-misconception"></a>The Misconception

- Most students (about 85%) believe that allotropy is a property unique to carbon, and that it refers to the ability of carbon to form long chains and rings.

- They often confuse allotropy with catenation, which is the ability of an element to form long chains.

- This misconception leads to a lack of understanding of the different allotropes of carbon, such as diamond, graphite, and fullerenes.

### <a id="the-reality"></a>The Reality

- The reality is that allotropy refers to the existence of an element in two or more different forms, which can have distinct physical properties.

- Carbon exhibits allotropy due to its ability to form $sp^2$ and $sp^3$ hybridized orbitals, resulting in different crystal structures.

- The different allotropes of carbon, such as diamond and graphite, have unique properties due to their distinct crystal structures.

### <a id="the-diagnostic-question"></a>The Diagnostic Question
What is the primary reason for the existence of different allotropes of carbon?

- A) The ability of carbon to form long chains

- B) The ability of carbon to form $sp^2$ and $sp^3$ hybridized orbitals

- C) The ability of carbon to form rings

- D) The ability of carbon to form double bonds

- If you answered A: you have the misconception → fix: allotropy is a result of the ability of carbon to form different hybridized orbitals, not just long chains.

- If you answered B: you are in the top 5% → now extend this: the $sp^2$ hybridized orbitals in graphite result in a planar, hexagonal structure, while the $sp^3$ hybridized orbitals in diamond result in a tetrahedral structure.

### <a id="how-to-never-forget-this"></a>How to Never Forget This
To remember the concept of allotropy in carbon, use the mnemonic "Hybrid Orbitals Result In Different Structures" (HORIDS).

- $sp^2$ hybridized orbitals result in a planar, hexagonal structure (graphite)

- $sp^3$ hybridized orbitals result in a tetrahedral structure (diamond)

- This will help you to associate the different allotropes of carbon with their unique properties and structures.

By understanding the concept of allotropy in carbon and avoiding common misconceptions, you can gain a deeper appreciation for the unique properties of carbon and its compounds, and excel in your exams. Visit examcompass.web.app for more study resources and exam tips.

**Answer:** A) B and C are incorrect because the percentage yield of CO$_2$ is not $84%$ and $88%$. D is incorrect because the percentage yield of CO$_2$ is not $92%$.
