## <a id="-the-one-thing-most-students-get-wrong"></a>🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

{
 "heading": "🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong",
 "body": "

- **The Core Concept:** The biggest pitfall in 'Comparing Quantities' for [class](/blog/compiler-design-class-12-notes) 8 isn't just calculating percentages, but understanding **successive percentage changes** where the *base value itself changes* after each operation. It's about how an initial quantity is modified by a percentage, and then that *new* modified quantity is further modified by another percentage.

- **What 85% scorers do:**
 

- Many students fall into the trap of simply adding or subtracting percentages directly when multiple changes occur. For instance, if a price increases by $10\%$ and then decreases by $10\%$, they might assume there's no overall change or that the net change is $0\%$. This is incorrect.
 

- Another common mistake is applying all percentage changes to the *original* amount. They might calculate the first percentage on the original, then the second percentage also on the original, and then add/subtract these absolute changes. This method is flawed because the base for the second percentage change has already shifted.
 

- Example: If an item's price is UP, and it increases by $10\%$ then by $20\%$, an 85% scorer might incorrectly think the total increase is $10\% + 20\% = 30\%$ of UP, leading to a final price of UP + 0.30P = 1.30P$. This is a major conceptual error.

- **What 95% scorers do:**
 

- They grasp that each successive percentage change operates on the *new value* obtained after the previous change, not the initial value. They understand that the base for calculation keeps shifting.
 

- They use a **multiplier approach** which is both accurate and efficient:
 

- For an ex\%$ increase, the multiplier is $(1 + x/100)$.
 

- For an ex\%$ decrease, the multiplier is $(1 

- x/100)$.
 

- To find the final amount after successive changes, they multiply the original amount by each individual multiplier in sequence.
 

- **Formula for successive changes:** If an original quantity HQ_{original}$ undergoes a UP_1\%$ change (increase/decrease) followed by a UP_2\%$ change (increase/decrease), the final quantity HQ_{final}$ is:
 BBQ_{final} = Q_{original} \times \left(1 \pm \franc{P_1}{100}\right) \times \left(1 \pm \franc{P_2}{100}\right)
 (Use '+' for increase, '-' for decrease).
 

- **Example:** If an item's price is UP, and it increases by $10\%$ then by $20\%$, a 95% scorer calculates:
 

- First change: UP \times (1 + 10/100) = P \times 1.10$
 

- Second change (on the new base): $(P \times 1.10) \times (1 + 20/100) = P \times 1.10 \times 1.20$
 

- Final price: UP \times 1.32 = 1.32P$.
 

- The net increase is $32$}
