## ⚡ Formula Bank

$pK_{a} = -\log_{10}[H^{+}]$ : Acid dissociation constant, 
 $pK_{b} = -\log_{10}[OH^{-}]$ : Base dissociation constant,

 $pOH = -\log_{10}[OH^{-}]$ : Measure of basicity, 
 $K_{w} = [H^{+}][OH^{-}] = 10^{-14}$ : Water dissociation constant, 
 $K_{a} = \frac{[H^{+}][A^{-}]}{[HA]}$ : Acid dissociation constant expression, 
 $K_{b} = \frac{[BH^{+}][OH^{-}]}{[B]}$ : Base dissociation constant expression, 
 $K_{a} \times K_{b} = K_{w}$ : Relationship between acid and base dissociation constants, 
 $pK_{a} + pK_{b} = 14$ : Relationship between acid and base dissociation constants in terms of pK, 
 $\frac{K_{b}}{K_{a}} = \frac{[BH^{+}]}{[A^{-}]}$ : Comparison of base and acid dissociation constants, 
 $K_{a} = \frac{[H^{+}][A^{-}]}{[HA]} = \frac{[H^{+}]}{K_{b}}$ : Relationship between acid dissociation constant and hydrogen ion concentration, 
 $pH = \frac{1}{2}pK_{w} + \frac{1}{2}\log_{10}\frac{[salt]}{[acid]}$ : Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for pH calculation, 
 $pOH = \frac{1}{2}pK_{w} + \frac{1}{2}\log_{10}\frac{[salt]}{[base]}$ : Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for pOH calculation, 
 $K_{sp} = [A^{n+}][B^{m-}]^{n}$ : Solubility product constant for the dissolution of a salt, 
 $K_{f} = \frac{[A^{n+}][B^{m-}]}{[AB_{m}^{n}]}$ : Formation constant for the formation of a complex ion, 
 $K_{d} = \frac{1}{K_{f}}$ : Instability constant for the dissociation of a complex ion, 
 $\Delta G^{\circ} = -RT\ln K$ : Relationship between Gibbs free energy and equilibrium constant, 
 $\Delta G^{\circ} = -2.303RT\log K$ : Relationship between Gibbs free energy and equilibrium constant, 
 $E^{\circ}_{cell} = \frac{\Delta G^{\circ}}{-nF}$ : Relationship between cell potential and Gibbs free energy, 
 $E^{\circ}_{cell} = \frac{RT}{nF}\ln K$ : Relationship between cell potential and equilibrium constant, 
 $E^{\circ}_{cell} = \frac{0.0591}{n}\log K$ : Relationship between cell potential and equilibrium constant at 25°C, 
 $E_{cell} = E^{\circ}_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{n}\log_{10}Q$ : Nernst equation for cell potential calculation, 
 $\log_{10}K = \frac{\Delta H^{\circ}}{2.303R}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}} - \frac{1}{T_{1}}\right)$ : Van 't Hoff equation for temperature dependence of equilibrium constant,

## 🪤 The 5 Mistakes That Cost Marks

Mistake 1: Not considering the stoichiometry of the reaction when calculating the amount of substance in a chemical reaction, specifically in the context of Chemistry in Everyday Life, such as in the production of soaps and detergents. 
Costs: Full 4 marks 
Fix: Always balance the chemical equation and use the mole ratio to calculate the amount of substance. For example, in the reaction of fatty acids with sodium hydroxide to produce soap: $\mathrm{C_{17}H_{35}COOH + NaOH ightarrow C_{17}H_{35}COONa + H_2O}$, ensure that the number of moles of fatty acid reacts with an equal number of moles of sodium hydroxide.

Mistake 2: Incorrectly applying the concept of concentration units, particularly when dealing with molarity ($\mathrm{M}$) and molality ($\mathrm{m}$), in problems related to the preparation of medicinal solutions.
Costs: Up to 3 marks 
Fix: Understand that $\mathrm{M} = \frac{\mathrm{moles\ of\ solute}}{\mathrm{liters\ of\ solution}}$ and $\mathrm{m} = \frac{\mathrm{moles\ of\ solute}}{\mathrm{kilograms\ of\ solvent}}$. For instance, when preparing a saline solution, the correct units must be used to avoid errors in calculating the amount of solute needed.

Mistake 3: Not accounting for the pH of the solution when determining the stability of certain compounds in everyday products, such as food preservatives and pharmaceuticals.
Costs: Up to 2 marks 
Fix: Recognize that pH affects the stability and reactivity of many compounds. For example, the stability of ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) is highly dependent on pH, with it being more stable in acidic conditions ($\mathrm{pH < 7}$). Use the $\mathrm{pH}$ formula: $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H}^+]$, to calculate the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution.

Mistake 4: Incorrectly identifying the type of chemical bond or intermolecular force present in a substance, which is crucial for understanding the properties of materials used in everyday life, such as the stickiness of adhesives or the strength of plastics.
Costs: Full 4 marks 
Fix: Be able to distinguish between ionic, covalent, and metallic bonds, as well as intermolecular forces like hydrogen bonding, dipole-dipole interactions, and London dispersion forces. For example, in the context of adhesives, understanding that the strength of the adhesive comes from intermolecular forces between the adhesive and the surfaces it binds to is crucial.

Mistake 5: Not applying the principles of chemical kinetics, specifically the rate of reaction and the effect of catalysts, to real-world scenarios, such as the decomposition of pollutants in the environment or the efficiency of catalytic converters in vehicles.
Costs: Up to 5 marks 
Fix: Recall that the rate of reaction can be influenced by factors like concentration, surface area, temperature, and the presence of catalysts. The rate equation is given by: $\mathrm{rate} = \mathrm{k}[\mathrm{A}]^m[\mathrm{B}]^n$, where $\mathrm{k}$ is the rate constant, and $\mathrm{m}$ and $\mathrm{n}$ are the orders of reaction with respect to reactants $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$, respectively. Catalysts lower the activation energy ($\mathrm{E_a}$) of the reaction without being consumed, thus increasing the rate of reaction without altering the equilibrium constant ($\mathrm{K}$).

## ✏️ 3 Solved PYQs

Q: What is the role of sodium benzoate in food products? Why is it used as a preservative? 
 Trap in this question: Students often get confused between the role of sodium benzoate and other preservatives. 
 Solution: Sodium benzoate is used as a preservative in food products because it can prevent the growth of microbes, particularly bacteria and fungi, by disrupting their cell membranes. The $pK_a$ value of benzoic acid is $4.19$. In the human body, the $pH$ is around $7.4$, so the benzoate ion is the predominant form of sodium benzoate. Using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: $pH = pK_a + \log\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$, where $[A^-]$ is the concentration of the conjugate base (benzoate ion) and $[HA]$ is the concentration of the weak acid (benzoic acid). Given that the $pH$ of the human body is $7.4$ and the $pK_a$ of benzoic acid is $4.19$, we can calculate the ratio of $[A^-]$ to $[HA]$ as $\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]} = 10^{(7.4-4.19)}$. 
 Answer: The role of sodium benzoate is to prevent the growth of microbes in food products, and it does so by disrupting their cell membranes, with a $pK_a$ value of $4.19$ and a ratio of $[A^-]$ to $[HA]$ of $10^{3.21}$.
 
 Q: An antacid tablet contains $m$ moles of $CaCO_3$ and $n$ moles of $Mg(OH)_2$. It is required to neutralize a minimum of $10 \times 10^{-2}$ moles of $HCl$ after reacting with $10 \times 10^{-2}$ moles of $H_2SO_4$. What is the minimum amount of $CaCO_3$ and $Mg(OH)_2$ required in $1$ antacid tablet? 
 Trap in this question: Students often get confused between the reactions of $CaCO_3$ and $Mg(OH)_2$ with $HCl$ and $H_2SO_4$. 
 Solution: First, we need to calculate the moles of $HCl$ and $H_2SO_4$ that need to be neutralized: $H_2SO_4 + 2HCl \rightarrow 2H_2O + SO_2 + Cl_2$ and $CaCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2$ and $Mg(OH)_2 + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + 2H_2O$. We can calculate the moles of $CaCO_3$ and $Mg(OH)_2$ required as $m = 10 \times 10^{-2}$ and $n = 10 \times 10^{-2}$. 
 Answer: The minimum amount of $CaCO_3$ and $Mg(OH)_2$ required is $10 \times 10^{-2}$ moles each.
 
 Q: A mixture of chloroform and acetone has a boiling point of $56^\circ C$. The vapour pressure of pure chloroform and acetone at $56^\circ C$ are $1.2 \times 10^5 Pa$ and $0.95 \times 10^5 Pa$ respectively. What is the mole fraction of chloroform in the mixture? 
 Trap in this question: Students often get confused between the vapour pressures of the pure components and the mixture. 
 Solution: We can use Raoult's law: $p = p_1^0 x_1 + p_2^0 x_2$, where $p$ is the total pressure, $p_1^0$ and $p_2^0$ are the vapour pressures of the pure components, and $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the mole fractions of the components. Let $x_1$ be the mole fraction of chloroform. Then $x_2 = 1 - x_1$. We know that $p = 1 \times 10^5 Pa$, $p_1^0 = 1.2 \times 10^5 Pa$, and $p_2^0 = 0.95 \times 10^5 Pa$. Substituting these values into Raoult's law, we get $1 \times 10^5 = 1.2 \times 10^5 x_1 + 0.95 \times 10^5 (1 - x_1)$. Solving for $x_1$, we get $x_1 = \frac{0.05}{0.25} = 0.2$. 
 Answer: The mole fraction of chloroform in the mixture is $0.2$.

## 🧠 The One Thing Most Students Get Wrong

When it comes to 'Chemistry in Everyday Life', one concept that separates 85% scorers from 95% scorers is the understanding of $\Delta G$ and its relation to the feasibility of a reaction in the context of chemical manufacturing and pharmaceutical applications. Most students struggle with applying the equation $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ to real-world scenarios, particularly in determining whether a reaction is spontaneous or not. The key here is recognizing that a negative $\Delta G$ indicates a spontaneous reaction, which is crucial for designing efficient chemical synthesis pathways. However, the interplay between $\Delta H$ (enthalpy change), $T$ (temperature in Kelvin), and $\Delta S$ (entropy change) is often misunderstood. For instance, in the production of ammonia via the Haber-Bosch process, the reaction is non-spontaneous under standard conditions (positive $\Delta G$), but becomes feasible at high pressures and low temperatures, which alters $\Delta G$ by affecting the $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ terms. High scorers understand how to manipulate these conditions to favor a negative $\Delta G$, thereby making the reaction spontaneous and industrially viable. Moreover, they can apply this understanding to predict the outcomes of reactions under various conditions, a skill that is frequently tested in exams. Thus, mastering the concept of $\Delta G$ and its application to real-world chemical processes is pivotal for excelling in this chapter.


| Concept | Key Points | Industrial Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\Delta G$ and Spontaneity | Negative $\Delta G$ indicates spontaneity, $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ | Designing efficient chemical synthesis pathways |
| Haber-Bosch Process | High pressure and low temperature favor spontaneity | Ammonia production |
| Manipulating $\Delta G$ | Altering $\Delta H$, $T$, and $\Delta S$ to achieve negative $\Delta G$ | Making non-spontaneous reactions industrially viable |

## 🔁 Last 5 Minutes Box

Last-minute revision for Chemistry in Everyday Life involves focusing on key concepts and formulas that are frequently tested. The following points are crucial to revisit:
 
 **Key Formulas:**
 * $K_{a} = \frac{[H^{+}][A^{-}]}{[HA]}$ for acid dissociation
 * $pH = -\log[H^{+}]$ for calculating pH
 * $pOH = -\log[OH^{-}]$ for calculating pOH
 * $pH + pOH = 14$ at 25°C for the relationship between pH and pOH

 **Important Facts:**
 * Soaps are salts of fatty acids and are used for cleaning purposes due to their ability to emulsify oils.

 * Not considering the temperature when calculating pH or pOH, as the $K_{w}$ value changes with temperature.
 * Forgetting that the $K_{a}$ value for an acid is a measure of its strength, with lower $K_{a}$ values indicating weaker acids.
 
 Understanding and remembering these key points will significantly enhance your performance in the Chemistry in Everyday Life section of the exam. Make sure to review them carefully and be prepared to apply the concepts and formulas to solve problems efficiently.

## 📝 Practice MCQs


**1. Which of the following is an example of a soap?**
Sodium lauryl sulphate
Sodium lauryl ether sulphate
Sodium stearate
All of the above

**Answer:** C) Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of higher fatty acids, so sodium stearate is a soap.


**2. Why are food preservatives used?**
To increase the shelf life of food
To decrease the shelf life of food
To change the taste of food
To change the colour of food

**Answer:** A) Food preservatives are used to prevent the growth of microorganisms and increase the shelf life of food.


**3. What is the role of an antiseptic?**
To kill microorganisms
To prevent the growth of microorganisms
To cure diseases
To prevent the growth of higher organisms

**Answer:** B) Antiseptics prevent the growth of microorganisms and are applied to living tissues.


**4. Which of the following is an example of a disinfectant?**
Dettol
Mouthwash
Soap
All of the above

**Answer:** A) Disinfectants are used to kill microorganisms and are applied to non-living surfaces, Dettol is a common disinfectant.


**5. What is the purpose of using emulsifying agents in medicines?**
To increase the solubility of the medicine
To decrease the solubility of the medicine
To mix two immiscible liquids
To separate two immiscible liquids

**Answer:** C) Emulsifying agents are used to mix two immiscible liquids, for example, oil and water, and are commonly used in medicines.
